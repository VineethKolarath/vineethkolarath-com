Think about how you learned to ride a bicycle.

Someone may have explained it to you. Keep your balance. Look ahead. Pedal steadily. Those instructions are all correct. But they probably did not help much until you actually got on the seat and tried it yourself.

You had to feel the bicycle move beneath you. You had to make small adjustments, find your balance and try again. Perhaps someone held the back of the seat while you practised. Gradually, you began to understand something that no explanation could quite give you.

You learnt by doing.

Many of the most useful things we know are like this. And I sometimes wonder whether, surrounded by so many explanations today, we are forgetting the importance of experience.

## Explanations are not the same as experience

We are surrounded by explanations.

A video can show us how a circuit works. A website can describe how a plant grows. An AI assistant can explain almost any chapter in simple language, at any hour, as many times as we like.

All of this is useful. I would not want to give it up.

But an explanation is not the same as having experienced something yourself. It is a map. It can show you where the road goes, but it cannot walk it for you.

When you read about how a circuit works, you follow the logic. When you build one, you meet the small problems that come with real components.

A wire is loose. The LED is connected the wrong way round. The reading is different from what you expected. Something that looked simple on the page refuses to work on the table.

The real world does not always behave as neatly as an explanation. And when it does not, you have to observe, think and make a decision.

That is where another kind of learning begins.

## What the real world teaches

Imagine a student who has learnt that an LED needs a suitable current-limiting resistor in series with it. She understands the reason and can explain it in an examination.

Now she builds a simple battery-powered circuit. The LED does not light up.

What should she do?

She could start changing everything. Replace the battery, move the wires, change the resistor and try again.

Or she could slow down and check one thing at a time.

Is the battery connected properly? Is there a complete path for the current? Is the LED facing the right direction? Is the resistor suitable for the circuit? Is there a loose connection?

She checks the connections and discovers that the LED was connected the wrong way round. She turns it around. This time, it lights up.

What has she learnt?

She has learnt that LEDs have a direction. She has seen how a loose connection can interrupt a circuit. She has discovered that her first assumption may be wrong. And she has experienced why checking one thing at a time is often more useful than changing everything at once.

These are small lessons, but they stay with you because you have worked through the problem yourself.

A textbook may explain the principle. Building the circuit gives you a chance to see what that principle means in practice.

This is also why I called mistakes useful information in [Mistakes Are Data](/writing/mistakes-are-data). A mistake you can investigate, test and correct gives you something concrete to learn from.

## What research suggests

There is research that supports the value of active participation in learning. But it is worth being careful about what the findings actually show.

A major study published in 2014 in the journal *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* analysed 225 studies involving undergraduate courses in science, technology, engineering and mathematics.

The researchers compared traditional lectures with active-learning approaches. These included activities in which students took part in solving problems, discussing ideas and working through questions rather than simply listening.

On average, students in active-learning classes performed better in examinations and were less likely to fail than students in traditional lecture-based classes.

This is an important finding. But it is about active learning in university STEM courses. It is not direct proof that building a physical object always produces better learning.

You can read the study here: [Active Learning Increases Student Performance in Science, Engineering, and Mathematics](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111).

There is also research into learning by drawing.

When students draw a diagram to explain an idea, they have to decide what belongs in the picture, how the different parts relate and what should connect to what. That process can help them organise their understanding.

But the evidence is not as simple as saying that drawing always improves learning. Research reviews have found that the results depend on the task, the subject and the support students receive. A carefully designed drawing activity can be useful; an activity that is too difficult or poorly guided may not help much.

The important point is not that every student must draw or build something for every lesson.

It is that learning can become deeper when students actively work with an idea instead of only receiving an explanation.

Doing something without thinking about it teaches very little. Doing it, observing what happens and reflecting on the result can teach a great deal.

## "Hands" does not only mean electronics

I write about engineering because it is my field. But thinking with your hands is much wider than circuits.

A student who draws a map of her village from memory is thinking with her hands.

So is one who builds a model of a cell from clay, tests which soil holds water longest in three small pots, writes a short programme or repairs a bicycle chain.

Someone who cooks a dish and adjusts the recipe is doing it too. So is a student who sketches how a bridge carries weight.

These activities may look very different, but they share something.

You try something. You see what happens. You notice what is different from what you expected. Then you decide what to change.

Try. Notice. Change.

It is a simple cycle, but it is one of the ways people learn to understand the world around them.

It is not less intellectual than reading. It is another way of using the mind.

## Why this matters more now

In [Learn the Basics Before the Tools](/writing/learn-the-basics-before-the-tools), I argued that tools help most when you understand what lies underneath them.

Making things is one way to build that understanding.

An AI assistant can explain how an electric motor works. It can even write the code to control one.

But when you connect that motor to a real wheel, things become less predictable. There may be friction. The shaft may be loose. The battery may not supply enough power. The motor may behave differently under load.

A student who has worked through these problems is better placed to ask the AI assistant a useful question. More importantly, that student has some experience against which to judge the answer.

If the assistant suggests something that does not match what is happening on the table, the student has a reason to question it.

In that sense, practical experience gives us a useful foundation for working with AI.

As I wrote in [What Is a Thinking Machine?](/writing/what-is-a-thinking-machine), a machine can produce a fluent answer that is wrong. Understanding how something works in the real world gives us another way to recognise when an explanation may not be right.

There is a quieter point too.

When answers come easily, it is tempting to stay in the world of words. We can keep reading, watching and asking for explanations without ever trying anything ourselves.

Making something pulls us back into the world of things, where effort, patience and small corrections still matter.

## Start small

If this sounds like a lot, it need not be.

In [Why Your First Project Should Be Small](/writing/why-your-first-project-should-be-small), I wrote about the value of making one small thing that works.

The same idea applies here.

You do not need a laboratory. You do not need expensive equipment or a complicated project.

A sheet of paper and a pencil may be enough. So might a few household materials, a simple model made from cardboard, a small battery-powered circuit or a basic experiment with soil and water.

You could even take something you have learnt in a textbook and draw a diagram that explains how its different parts fit together.

Safety matters, of course. Use suitable materials and equipment. For beginner electronics, stick to simple battery-powered, low-voltage kits with appropriate components. Do not experiment with mains electricity, open power supplies or dismantle unfamiliar appliances. Ask a trusted adult for help when a project involves tools, heat, chemicals or anything you are unsure about.

Curiosity should never come at the cost of getting hurt.

India has also recognised the value of giving students opportunities to experiment.

The Atal Innovation Mission supports [Atal Tinkering Labs](https://aim.gov.in/atl-overview.php) in schools. Their purpose is to encourage curiosity, creativity and innovation by giving students opportunities to work with tools and technologies.

Where such a lab exists in a school, it is worth asking how students can use it.

Where it does not, a table, a few materials and some time can still do a great deal.

## A word to parents and teachers

Making things is messy.

Paper gets cut. Wires get tangled. Models collapse. Sometimes, a student spends an hour on something that still does not work.

It is tempting to hurry past this and return to something tidier, like the textbook or the examination.

But sometimes the most useful part of an activity is the moment when the first attempt does not work.

Instead of immediately giving the student the answer, ask:

"What did you expect to happen?"

"What actually happened?"

"What could be causing the difference?"

"What would you like to try next?"

These questions encourage students to observe and think rather than simply wait for instructions.

Let them try before explaining everything, when it is safe to do so. Value the reasoning and the process, not only the finished object. And allow an unsuccessful attempt to be a normal part of learning, not a reason for embarrassment.

At home, a corner of a table and a box of simple materials can become a small workshop.

Ask a child to show you how something works. Build a paper model together. Try a small experiment. Let them explain what they noticed.

You may be surprised by how much they understand once they have the opportunity to work with an idea themselves.

None of this competes with examinations. Practical experience can help students remember and apply what they learn, although it does not replace reading, explanation or examination practice.

And even beyond examination results, there is something valuable about discovering that you can make something with your own hands.

## Try this

Choose one idea that you have learnt only by reading or watching this month.

It could be from science, mathematics, geography or any other subject.

Now spend one hour making something that helps you explore that idea.

It could be a drawing of how different parts connect, a small model made from paper or clay, a simple experiment, a short programme or a basic circuit using a safe battery-powered kit.

While you work, keep a few notes:

- What did I expect to happen?
- What actually happened?
- What surprised me?
- What did I have to change?
- What would I try differently next time?

Afterwards, compare what you understood before the activity with what you understand now.

You may discover that something you thought was clear was not quite so clear. Or you may find that an idea which seemed difficult becomes much easier once you can see how its parts fit together.

In my first essay, [Bring a Rough Idea](/writing/bring-a-rough-idea), I asked you to bring a rough idea.

This time, bring something you have made.

It does not have to be beautiful. It does not have to work perfectly. It can be rough, unfinished or even a little broken.

What matters is what you noticed while making it.

Some things are easier to understand when you read about them.

Others become clear only when you try them yourself.
